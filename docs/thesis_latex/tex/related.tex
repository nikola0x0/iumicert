\chapter{BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK}
\label{ch:background-related-work}

This chapter provides the theoretical foundation necessary for understanding the IU-MiCert system and contextualizes the research within existing literature. The chapter is organized into three main sections: first, theoretical background covering the cryptographic and blockchain primitives used in the system; second, a survey of current advancements in blockchain-based credential systems; and third, identification of research gaps that motivate the IU-MiCert approach.

\section{Theoretical Background}
This section establishes the foundational concepts necessary for understanding the IU-MiCert protocol, building from blockchain fundamentals through advanced cryptographic primitives to Verkle tree structures.

\subsection{Blockchain Technology}
\label{subsec:blockchain_technology}

Blockchain technology provides the foundational layer for decentralized, immutable credential storage. The core properties of blockchain systems make them particularly suitable for academic credential management.

\textbf{Immutability} ensures that once data is written to the blockchain, it cannot be altered or deleted. This property is achieved through cryptographic hash chaining, where each block contains a hash of the previous block, creating a tamper-evident chain. Any modification to historical data would require recomputing all subsequent blocks, which is computationally infeasible in a properly secured blockchain.

\textbf{Consensus mechanisms} enable distributed agreement on the blockchain state without requiring a central authority. Ethereum, the platform used in this thesis, employs Proof-of-Stake consensus where validators secure the network by staking capital. This decentralized consensus ensures that no single entity can unilaterally modify credential records.

\textbf{Transparency} allows all transactions to be publicly verifiable. Anyone can query the blockchain to verify that a term root commitment was published at a specific time by a specific issuer. This transparency builds trust while maintaining privacy, as only commitment roots (not actual credential data) are stored on-chain.

\textbf{Smart contracts} provide self-executing code that runs on the blockchain, enabling programmable verification logic. Smart contracts eliminate the need for intermediaries by automatically enforcing credential verification rules, reducing costs and enabling trustless verification.

\subsection{Ethereum Platform and Gas Economics}
\label{subsec:ethereum}

Ethereum serves as the deployment platform for IU-MiCert's smart contract infrastructure. Understanding Ethereum's gas economics is critical for designing cost-effective credential systems.

The cost of blockchain operations on Ethereum is measured in \textbf{gas}—a unit representing computational effort. Each operation (storage, computation, transaction) consumes a specific amount of gas. The total transaction cost is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\text{Transaction Cost} = \text{Gas Used} \times \text{Gas Price (in ETH)}
\end{equation}

For the IU-MiCert system, publishing a term root commitment requires approximately 204,000 gas based on actual deployment measurements. At a typical gas price of 20 gwei (20 × 10\textsuperscript{-9} ETH), the cost per term publication is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Cost per Term} = 204,000 \times 20 \times 10^{-9} \approx 0.004 \text{ ETH}
\end{equation}

This economic model has profound implications for system design. By storing only 32-byte root commitments on-chain rather than individual credential hashes, IU-MiCert achieves dramatic cost savings. A single term root covers all students and all courses in that term, amortizing the blockchain cost across potentially thousands of credentials. This design enables economically viable deployment at institutional scale.

\subsection{Pedersen Commitments}
\label{subsec:pedersen}

Pedersen commitments form a fundamental cryptographic primitive used in Verkle tree construction. A Pedersen commitment allows one to commit to a value without revealing it, while ensuring the commitment cannot later be changed to a different value.

Formally, a Pedersen commitment is defined as:

\begin{equation}
C = g^m \cdot h^r
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $g$ and $h$ are generator points on an elliptic curve
    \item $m$ is the message (value being committed)
    \item $r$ is a random blinding factor
    \item Operations occur in an elliptic curve group
\end{itemize}

Pedersen commitments possess two critical properties:

\textbf{Hiding property:} Given commitment $C$, it is computationally infeasible to determine the message $m$ without knowing the blinding factor $r$. This property relies on the discrete logarithm problem's hardness on the chosen elliptic curve.

\textbf{Binding property:} Once a commitment $C$ is published, the committer cannot find a different message $m'$ and blinding factor $r'$ such that $C = g^{m'} \cdot h^{r'}$. This ensures the commitment cannot be changed after publication.

In Verkle trees, Pedersen commitments are used to commit to the 256 child values at each node. This enables compact representation of tree nodes while maintaining cryptographic security.

\subsection{Inner Product Argument (IPA)}
\label{subsec:ipa}

Inner Product Arguments provide the cryptographic proof system underlying Verkle tree verification. IPA enables proving polynomial evaluations without revealing the polynomial itself, which is essential for maintaining privacy while enabling verification.

The fundamental problem IPA solves is: given a commitment $C$ to a polynomial $P(X)$, an evaluation point $z$, and a claimed result $y$, prove that $P(z) = y$ without revealing the polynomial $P$.

The IPA protocol achieves this through a logarithmic number of interaction rounds, producing proofs of size O(log $n$) where $n$ is the polynomial degree. Each round halves the problem size through a folding technique, similar to binary search.

\textbf{Why IPA over KZG?} The choice between Inner Product Arguments and Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg (KZG) commitments represents a fundamental design decision in credential systems:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|p{3cm}|p{5.5cm}|p{4.5cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Factor} & \textbf{KZG} & \textbf{IPA} \\
\hline
Trusted Setup & Required (multi-party ceremony) & Not needed \\
\hline
Proof Size & O(1) - 48 bytes & O(log n) - approximately 1.8 KB \\
\hline
Verification & Fast (pairing operations) & Slower (scalar multiplications) \\
\hline
Security & If setup compromised, all proofs forgeable & No trusted setup risk \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Comparison of KZG and IPA Proof Systems}
\label{tab:kzg-ipa}
\end{table}

For IU-MiCert, the choice of IPA is driven by the trustless setup requirement. Academic credentials must remain valid for decades, potentially outliving institutions and administrative structures. A trusted setup ceremony creates a long-term security vulnerability: if the setup parameters are ever compromised, all historical credentials become forgeable. IPA eliminates this risk entirely, making it the appropriate choice for long-lived credential systems despite slightly larger proof sizes.

\subsection{Verkle Trees}
\label{subsec:verkle_trees}

Verkle trees represent the culmination of the cryptographic primitives discussed above, combining Pedersen commitments with IPA proofs to create an efficient tree structure for credential storage and verification.

A Verkle tree is a 256-ary tree (each node has up to 256 children) where each node stores a Pedersen commitment to its children's values. The tree structure is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:verkle-structure}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/verkle_tree_structure.png}
\caption{Verkle Tree Structure with Commitments and Proofs}
\label{fig:verkle-structure}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Key Innovation:} The 256-ary branching factor (versus binary in Merkle trees) provides wide trees with efficient access patterns. The go-verkle implementation uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Binary Merkle tree: 20 levels deep for 1M items
    \item 256-ary Verkle tree: 32 levels with 256-ary width per level
\end{itemize}

Each level reduction decreases proof size and verification time. However, the critical advantage is that Verkle proofs use IPA, resulting in constant-size proofs regardless of tree size.

\textbf{Proof Size Comparison:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Merkle tree (1M leaves): 32 bytes × 20 levels = 640 bytes
    \item Verkle tree (1M leaves): approximately 1.8 KB (constant!)
\end{itemize}

The proof size remains constant even as the tree grows to 10 million or 100 million entries, providing true O(1) scalability for credential verification.

\textbf{Application to Academic Credentials:} In IU-MiCert, each academic term has one Verkle tree containing all course completions for all students in that term. The 32-byte root commitment is published to the blockchain, providing an immutable anchor point. Students receive receipts containing the Verkle proofs for their specific courses, enabling selective disclosure—they can prove completion of individual courses without revealing their full academic record.

\subsection{Granularity Gap}
Existing systems such as BlockCerts and IU-TransCert can only verify complete certificates or transcripts. This whole-credential approach prevents verification of individual courses, which is increasingly important as education moves toward modular, skills-based models. Employers seeking to verify specific technical skills cannot do so without access to complete transcripts, creating unnecessary privacy exposure.

\subsection{Temporal Integrity Gap}
Current blockchain credential systems lack mechanisms to cryptographically prove when credentials were issued. While blockchain timestamps exist for the transaction that published a credential hash, there is no cryptographic binding between the credential content and the timestamp. This enables potential backdating attacks where credentials are created retroactively and anchored to blockchain with current timestamps, making it difficult to detect fraudulent timeline manipulation.

\subsection{Proof Efficiency Gap}
Merkle tree-based systems produce proofs that grow logarithmically with tree size (O(log n)). For institutions with hundreds of thousands of credentials, proof sizes can become unwieldy. More critically, as systems scale to millions of credentials, verification overhead increases. Verkle trees address this through constant-size proofs that remain efficient regardless of dataset size.

\subsection{Privacy Gap}
Existing systems require full credential disclosure for verification. If a student wants to prove completion of a single programming course, they must typically reveal their entire transcript to the verifier. This violates privacy principles and exposes sensitive academic information unnecessarily. The lack of selective disclosure mechanisms limits practical adoption in privacy-conscious contexts.

\subsection{Revocation Gap}
Credential revocation remains challenging in blockchain systems due to immutability. Some systems maintain separate revocation lists (requiring additional on-chain storage and verification overhead), while others simply lack revocation functionality. Neither approach is satisfactory for academic institutions that occasionally need to revoke credentials due to academic misconduct or administrative errors.

\subsection{Economic Scalability Gap}
Storing individual credential hashes on-chain creates costs that scale linearly with the number of credentials. For large institutions issuing thousands of credentials per term, blockchain costs become prohibitive. The lack of efficient aggregation mechanisms prevents cost-effective deployment at institutional scale.

IU-MiCert addresses all six gaps through its Verkle tree architecture, version-based revocation design, and term-based aggregation strategy, as detailed in subsequent chapters.
